Approach

How I work on under-formed opportunities.

Most strategic opportunities arrive in the wrong shape. They show up as a technology, a market signal, a partner conversation, or a project someone has already approved — rarely as a question that can be decided.

Below are four situations where that pattern repeats. They are problem types, not accounts of past engagements: each describes how I frame the question and what the work produces.

01

Multi-Jurisdiction Expansion Architecture

From overseas ambition to structured expansion logic.

View approachClose approach

A company with a working technology and a domestic base starts asking how to go abroad. The question usually arrives as market selection: which country first.

That framing is too small. The more useful question is what distinct role each jurisdiction plays — where the technology and industrial base stay, where coordination and headquarters functions sit, where manufacturing and market access get built, and what financing narrative each of those choices implies.

I work this by separating the functions from the geography, then testing which sequences are reversible and which are not. The output is an expansion logic that can be discussed with stakeholders, taken to partners, and advanced in phases — rather than a country ranking that forces a commitment on the first move.

02

Technology-to-System Reframing

From standalone capability to system-level relevance.

View approachClose approach

A strong technical capability gets understood too narrowly: one material, one product, one spec sheet. The commercial conversation then stalls, because the people who would have to adopt it cannot see where it fits.

The real question is not how to explain the technology better. It is where the technology could matter inside a larger system, who in that system carries the adoption decision, and what conditions would have to hold for it to become commercially and institutionally relevant.

I work this through application scenarios, integration logic, and deployment conditions, with particular attention to the boundary of what can credibly be claimed at an early stage. Overclaiming at that point is the most common way a sound technology loses a serious counterparty. The output is a basis for discussing commercialization and partnership that holds up under technical scrutiny.

03

Founder Intent & Institutional Continuity

From founder judgment to organizational continuity.

View approachClose approach

In founder-led organizations, a great deal of strategic judgment exists only in one person’s head, and only in that person’s phrasing. The organization runs on informal interpretation of it. That holds until succession, or scale, or the first decision that has to be made without the founder in the room.

This is not a culture-deck problem and not a founder profile. The question is which parts of that judgment are actually transferable, and what language and decision frameworks would let the organization apply them unsupervised.

I work this by separating intent from anecdote, then building continuity logic the organization can use. The output is a bridge between founder-level judgment and institutional decision-making.

04

Demonstration-to-Platform Design

From showcase project to replicable platform logic.

View approachClose approach

A demonstration project gets approved and the visible task becomes building it and showing it well. The strategic question underneath is what the demonstration is meant to prove, and to whom.

Treated as a showcase, it produces goodwill and little else. Treated as a structured initiative, it becomes a validation site, a stakeholder interface, a partner engagement tool, and the evidence base for replication.

I work this by defining the proof obligations first — what evidence has to be captured, which partners hold which roles, what would count as a negative result — and designing the demonstration backwards from those. The output is a pathway from one project to a replicable platform, with commitment boundaries visible at each step.

Background

I founded Meridian Strategy Lab in 2025, after roughly fifteen years inside China’s national research system, where the recurring problem was the one MSL is built around: real capability that had not yet been turned into a proposition anyone outside could act on.

My background is unusual in shape. I have moved horizontally rather than vertically, across legal, corporate, investment, and governance functions. That is a poor way to build a title and a good way to learn how a decision actually travels through an organization.

How I Work

Most under-formed opportunities stall at the seams between functions — the technical case is sound, the legal structure is unresolved, the governance path is unclear, and no one owns the translation between them. That translation is the work.

I work in cross-border settings where the two sides apply different standards of evidence, and much of what I do is making a proposition legible to a counterparty whose approval logic is not the client’s own.

Meridian Strategy Lab is a personal practice. Engaging MSL means working with me.
